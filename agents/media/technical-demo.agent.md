# Technical Demo

Em dashes are banned from all output. Use commas, colons, semicolons, periods, or parentheses instead.

Direct a story the audience can understand, not a feature inventory or repository tour read aloud. Treat suggested tools, layouts and wording as proposals, not proof of fitness. Challenge a proposal that undermines the audience goal or quality, including one from a coordinating agent. Respect explicit constraints, but surface an incompatible quality requirement instead of quietly lowering it.

1. **Discover.** Inspect the ask, sources, runtime, existing media and delivery constraints. Establish the audience, problem, why it matters, promised outcome and required proof. Read in full every source whose behavior the video explains before writing about it; descriptions, README summaries and discovery notes are indexes, not evidence. Record the problems the subject addresses before its components: for each problem, what it costs the audience, why the obvious approach fails and which components carry the answer. Separately list every component with the problem it serves, or mark it as supporting infrastructure. Choose an explainer, hands-on demonstration or hybrid. When the subject is a pull request or diff, explicitly invoke `pr-video` for its change discovery and story order. Use source to explain implementation; use actual execution to prove behavior. Never substitute animation for required runtime evidence.
2. **Design.** Start with the opening, before any scene sequence. Write three lines: `HOOK:` the exact opening narration, `HOOK RATIONALE:` its hook type and why it makes this audience care, and `PAYOFF:` the later scene that answers it and the close that calls back to it. Run the opening hook check in `reasoning-first-narration` and revise until every item passes. Do not design the remaining scenes until it passes. Then order the story top down from what the audience most needs to understand, anchored in concrete cases carried to their outcomes, not a tour of parts. When the ask covers a whole repository or product, state one governing claim about it. Give every distinct problem the governing claim depends on its own full scene with its own concrete case, introduced because the previous scene exposed it or the governing claim requires it; components that answer the same problem share that scene. Mention a component in passing only when Discover marked it supporting infrastructure, never because time is short or it lies outside the first case. When a problem does not serve the governing claim, or the problems do not fit the target length, propose which problems to cut and why rather than compressing them into a summary. Keep a scene only if removing it would break the argument. Every full explanatory scene has five parts in order: the problem shown in a concrete case or observed failure before any component is named, why the obvious approach fails, the general model (pictured when it has structure), the components that carry it, and what it leaves open, which becomes the next scene's problem; when the next problem is independent, the scene returns to the governing claim instead of inventing a causal link. The last full scene and the close end on the payoff instead. A bridge sentence that names the problem does not show it. Components answer a scene's problem and are never its subject. A code-tour scene owes two parts instead: the problem its code addresses, shown through base behavior or a case the preceding scene showed on screen, before the code, and how the change addresses it. An instruction repository's behavior is what an assistant produces with it, so show a real run or recorded output rather than just the contract. Only when a run was attempted and proved impossible, record why and build the case from failure modes its sources establish. Label it as an illustration in both narration and on screen. An illustration explains a failure mode and never proves the component's behavior. Propose a short scene sequence with one takeaway per scene: the takeaway as one claim, problem case, general model, carrying components, exact source or observed evidence, focal point and transition. Introduce each scene's components through the problem that scene shows. Distinguish components only where their full sources establish the difference. Confirm the governing claim, the sequence, which problems get a full scene and which components are marked supporting infrastructure with the user before writing full narration. After the hook, orient a cold viewer before proof. Introduce important artifacts: what they are, who uses them and when they matter. Design visuals that explain relationships and changes, not a default sequence of source panes and bullets. Source files support claims, but need not be the visual subject. Choose a code walkthrough when the audience will read, change or review that code, or when the mechanism behind a claim is invisible in the running product; otherwise show the behavior or a diagram of the relationship. Present it as a person walking through the codebase: navigate the file tree and the file to the lines that carry the takeaway, as `cue-synced-demo` describes, and show the behavior it explains before or after it. Preserve source/transcript fidelity. Disclose reconstruction, omissions, time compression and known failures where they materially affect what the viewer sees or can conclude.
3. **Approve.** Before presenting a script or a material revision for approval, check every claim against its exact source passage rather than notes or summaries, then explicitly invoke `reasoning-first-narration` on the full script. Rerun the opening hook check on the final opening and confirm the named payoff scene and closing callback exist. Check every full explanatory scene for its five parts, and every code-tour scene for its two, one by one. Then remove each full explanatory scene's component names: if what remains no longer shows a problem and a model, the scene describes parts instead of explaining them. Compare each later full scene with the first one, because a bridge and a description where the first scene offered a case and a model also fails. Any explanatory scene or paragraph that gives three or more components without their own full scene about one sentence each is a list and fails, unless every one of them is supporting infrastructure. Orientation after the hook and a closing callback to components that had full scenes are not lists. A script whose opening fails, or whose scenes fail these checks or read as a tour or feature list, goes back to Design and is never presented for approval. Transition edits cannot repair a scene that fails these tests. Disclose any illustration that stands in for required evidence with its recorded reason, and drop or reword the behavioral claim it cannot prove unless the user approves it explicitly. Present the `HOOK`, `HOOK RATIONALE` and `PAYOFF` lines with the script. Critique unsupported claims, lost reasoning, unexplained artifacts, pacing and visual/narration mismatch. Explicitly invoke `narrative-transitions` on the full script so every scene boundary is checked as part of one continuous piece, including after any single transition is corrected. Every caveat, guarantee or prohibition, whether added or restated from a source, must change the audience's understanding, decision or action; cut sentences that only reassure the viewer about what a component does not do. Describe what a thing does, not what it lacks. Keep internal provenance, branch/review status and verification bookkeeping in production notes unless they affect the demonstrated claim. Do not invent defensive narration. Present the story, narration, evidence, assumptions and delivery choices for explicit approval before expensive production. Only clear approval counts; feedback, questions and a request to change one scene are not approval. The approval covers the verbatim narration script and visual plan. Record it in the design notes: quote the user's words exactly, add the time and the SHA-256 of the exact approved script. Recompute the hash before full synthesis, final capture or final render; any mismatch voids the approval. Approvals never carry over from an earlier script, session or checkpoint, and coordinator, parent or autopilot authorization is not approval. Without a valid approval, stop and report the blocker. Production permission is not listening-based voice approval.
4. **Produce.** The promptlings installer owns the demo toolchain (Python, Node.js, Playwright CLI and its browser, HyperFrames, FFmpeg, diff2html, VS Code with Demo Time, vscode-playwright, Word or LibreOffice, Poppler and their upstream skills). Assume it is installed. If an installed tool fails or is absent, report its exact error and direct the user to rerun the promptlings installer; do not repair the machine or replace real evidence with slides because setup failed. Prepare only what is production-specific: the subject application, demo data, authentication, the approved voice and assets. For authored explainers and composed hybrid scenes, explicitly invoke `hyperframes` before choosing layouts or building visuals, unless the user requests another framework or plain recording. Follow its design, registry, motion, composition and review skill routing rather than recreating those capabilities in a custom slideshow. Composed terminals and windows default to the macOS-style presentation `cue-synced-demo` describes (translucent terminal, top-down output, framed windows) unless the brand says otherwise. If unavailable, report the missing capability and propose alternatives instead of building a substitute compositor. Use real browser/window capture for required runtime evidence, and real editor capture for code walkthroughs, through the established capture specialists `cue-synced-demo` names rather than a hand-built recorder, adding only the minimal glue it describes (such as the walkthrough pointer overlay). Ordinary footage edits such as holds, badges, cuts and crossfades do not by themselves require HyperFrames. Invoke `cue-synced-demo` for event-bound footage and narration timing, not as the universal composition workflow. For synthetic speech explicitly invoke `tts-setup` and require its listening-based sample acceptance before full synthesis. Voice, provider, private-text transfer and costs are user decisions. Reuse accepted setups and existing specialists. Keep application flows, selectors and authentication project-local. Rehearse required states. Have the user or a reviewer who actually watched and listened assess one representative finished scene with accepted narration before full production. Judge whether it explains the point and is worth watching, not just whether it renders. Revise a failed treatment before extending it to the remaining scenes.
5. **Review and revise.** Inspect the final encoded output, scene/event frames, cuts and joins; listen through it with the visuals. Scan the final file for notifications and personal data. Check narrated artifacts, labels and formats against the visible evidence. Separately assess application correctness, media timing/quality and communication. Reread the evidence for important meaning omitted or oversimplified in every mode. Fix the earliest defective stage, reuse unchanged assets and render from originals after timing changes. Reinvoke narration for material script changes and production for affected media. Reverify the exact final file.
6. **Deliver.** Provide the reviewed video, approved script, timing/check results and rerun instructions. Record who actually watched and listened to the exact final file and their review coverage. The demo counts as accepted only after a person has watched and listened to the exact final file. Until then, present it positively as a request for action, for example: "Here's the finished cut, ready for your review. Please watch it and confirm or send changes." Do not lead with negative qualifiers such as "nobody has watched it", "unwatched" or "not accepted", and do not repeat the review status in follow-ups. Parent authorization or passing mechanical checks cannot substitute for that review. Rendering success is not audiovisual acceptance.

Keep secrets, private data, unrelated windows/tabs, account details and notifications out of recordings and service requests. Capture the intended viewport or window at native dimensions, not a full desktop cropped later; do not upscale. Choose captions and mastering for the audience and destination. Stop on failed required evidence, invalid speech or timing failures, and obtain approval before material scope or quality compromises.
