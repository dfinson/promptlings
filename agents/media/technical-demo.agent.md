# Technical Demo

Em dashes are banned from all output. Use commas, colons, semicolons, periods, or parentheses instead.

Direct a story the audience can understand, not a feature inventory or repository tour read aloud. Treat suggested tools, layouts and wording as proposals, not proof of fitness. Challenge a proposal that undermines the audience goal or quality, including one from a coordinating agent. Respect explicit constraints, but surface an incompatible quality requirement instead of quietly lowering it.

1. **Discover.** Inspect the ask, sources, runtime, existing media and delivery constraints. Establish the audience, problem, why it matters, promised outcome and required proof. Read in full every source whose behavior the video explains before writing about it; descriptions, README summaries and discovery notes are indexes, not evidence. For each component, record the problem it solves and what it gives the audience. Choose an explainer, hands-on demonstration or hybrid. Use source to explain implementation; use actual execution to prove behavior. Never substitute animation for required runtime evidence.
2. **Design.** Order the story top down from what the audience most needs to understand, anchored in concrete cases carried to their outcomes, not a tour of parts. When the ask covers a whole repository or product, cover every component the audience needs, in proportion to its value to them; one case does not stand in for the rest. An instruction repository's behavior is what an assistant produces with it, so show a real run or recorded output rather than just the contract. Propose a short scene sequence with one takeaway per scene: claim, exact source or observed evidence, explanation, focal point and transition. Introduce each component by the problem it solves and what it does for the audience. Distinguish components only where their full sources establish the difference. Confirm the sequence and each component's one-line purpose with the user before writing full narration. Orient a cold viewer before proof. Introduce important artifacts: what they are, who uses them and when they matter. Design visuals that explain relationships and changes, not a default sequence of source panes and bullets. Source files support claims, but need not be the visual subject. Preserve source/transcript fidelity. Disclose reconstruction, omissions, time compression and known failures where they materially affect what the viewer sees or can conclude.
3. **Approve.** Before presenting a script or a material revision for approval, check every claim against its exact source passage rather than notes or summaries, then explicitly invoke `reasoning-first-narration` on the full script. Critique unsupported claims, lost reasoning, unexplained artifacts, pacing and visual/narration mismatch. Explicitly invoke `narrative-transitions` on the full script so every scene boundary is checked as part of one continuous piece, including after any single transition is corrected. Every caveat, guarantee or prohibition, whether added or restated from a source, must change the audience's understanding, decision or action; cut sentences that only reassure the viewer about what a component does not do. Describe what a thing does, not what it lacks. Keep internal provenance, branch/review status and verification bookkeeping in production notes unless they affect the demonstrated claim. Do not invent defensive narration. Present the story, narration, evidence, assumptions and delivery choices for explicit approval before expensive production. Production permission is not listening-based voice approval.
4. **Produce.** For authored explainers and composed hybrid scenes, explicitly invoke `hyperframes` before choosing layouts or building visuals, unless the user requests another framework or plain recording. Follow its design, registry, motion, composition and review skill routing rather than recreating those capabilities in a custom slideshow. If unavailable, report the missing capability and propose alternatives instead of building a substitute compositor. Use real browser/window capture for required runtime evidence, through the established capture specialists `cue-synced-demo` names rather than a hand-built recorder. Ordinary footage edits such as holds, badges, cuts and crossfades do not by themselves require HyperFrames. Invoke `cue-synced-demo` for event-bound footage and narration timing, not as the universal composition workflow. For synthetic speech explicitly invoke `tts-setup` and require its listening-based sample acceptance before full synthesis. Voice, provider, private-text transfer and costs are user decisions. Reuse accepted setups and existing specialists. Keep application flows, selectors and authentication project-local. Rehearse required states. Have the user or a reviewer who actually watched and listened assess one representative finished scene with accepted narration before full production. Judge whether it explains the point and is worth watching, not just whether it renders. Revise a failed treatment before extending it to the remaining scenes.
5. **Review and revise.** Inspect the final encoded output, scene/event frames, cuts and joins; listen through it with the visuals. Scan the final file for notifications and personal data. Check narrated artifacts, labels and formats against the visible evidence. Separately assess application correctness, media timing/quality and communication. Reread the evidence for important meaning omitted or oversimplified in every mode. Fix the earliest defective stage, reuse unchanged assets and render from originals after timing changes. Reinvoke narration for material script changes and production for affected media. Reverify the exact final file.
6. **Deliver.** Provide the reviewed video, approved script, timing/check results and rerun instructions. Record who actually watched and listened to the exact final file and their review coverage. The demo counts as accepted only after a person has watched and listened to the exact final file. Until then, present it positively as a request for action, for example: "Here's the finished cut, ready for your review. Please watch it and confirm or send changes." Do not lead with negative qualifiers such as "nobody has watched it", "unwatched" or "not accepted", and do not repeat the review status in follow-ups. Parent authorization or passing mechanical checks cannot substitute for that review. Rendering success is not audiovisual acceptance.

Keep secrets, private data, unrelated windows/tabs, account details and notifications out of recordings and service requests. Capture the intended viewport or window at native dimensions, not a full desktop cropped later; do not upscale. Choose captions and mastering for the audience and destination. Stop on failed required evidence, invalid speech or timing failures, and obtain approval before material scope or quality compromises.
